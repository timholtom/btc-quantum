Shor's Algorithm Published

1994 — Peter Shor proves quantum computers could efficiently break the cryptographic foundations of modern security systems.

← Back to Timeline

What Happened

MIT mathematician Peter Shor published "Algorithms for quantum computation: discrete logarithms and factoring," fundamentally changing the landscape of cryptography and computer science. His algorithm demonstrated that a sufficiently powerful quantum computer could efficiently solve two mathematical problems that form the security foundation of most modern encryption systems: integer factorization and discrete logarithm computation.

The breakthrough showed that RSA encryption (based on factoring large integers) and elliptic curve cryptography (based on discrete logarithms) could be broken in polynomial time on a quantum computer, compared to the exponential time required by classical computers. This represented an exponential speedup that would render these cryptographic systems completely insecure once powerful enough quantum computers became available.

Shor's work built upon earlier quantum computing research by David Deutsch and Richard Feynman, but was the first to identify a practical application where quantum computers could dramatically outperform classical ones. The algorithm's publication marked the birth of the quantum computing field as we know it today, transforming quantum mechanics from theoretical physics into a potential engineering challenge with enormous implications.

How Shor's Algorithm Works

The algorithm exploits quantum superposition and quantum parallelism to find the period of a mathematical function much more efficiently than classical computers. For RSA encryption, Shor's algorithm finds the factors of large composite numbers by identifying periods in modular exponentiation. For elliptic curve cryptography (which Bitcoin uses), it solves the discrete logarithm problem by finding periods in the group structure of elliptic curves.

The key insight is that quantum computers can evaluate a function at many input values simultaneously through superposition, then use quantum interference to extract the period information. The Quantum Fourier Transform, a crucial component of Shor's algorithm, enables this extraction by converting the periodic structure into measurable quantum states.

While the mathematical details are complex, the practical result is clear: classical computers require exponential time to break these cryptographic systems (making them effectively unbreakable), while quantum computers running Shor's algorithm could break them in polynomial time (making them completely vulnerable). This represents one of the most dramatic computational advantages ever identified between different types of computing systems.

Long-Term Impact and Bitcoin's Vulnerability

Shor's algorithm created the theoretical foundation for what would later become known as the "quantum threat" to Bitcoin and other cryptocurrencies. Bitcoin relies on elliptic curve digital signatures (ECDSA and Schnorr) to secure transactions, both of which are vulnerable to Shor's algorithm. When powerful enough quantum computers become available, they could potentially derive private keys from public keys, compromising the entire security model of Bitcoin.

The publication also sparked the field of post-quantum cryptography, as researchers began developing new mathematical problems that would remain secure even against quantum computers. However, this quantum-resistant cryptography research would take decades to mature, creating a potential "crypto cliff" where existing systems could become vulnerable before replacements are widely deployed.

For Bitcoin specifically, Shor's algorithm identified two types of vulnerability: long-exposure attacks (against public keys already visible on the blockchain) and short-exposure attacks (against public keys revealed during transaction broadcast). The Bitcoin community's current quantum preparedness efforts, including BIP-360, can be traced directly back to the security implications that Shor identified in 1994.
