Caltech 6,100-Qubit Neutral Atom Array

September 2025 — Record-breaking quantum array operates at room temperature with 99.98% accuracy.

← Back to Timeline

What Happened

Scientists at Caltech, led by Professor Manuel Endres, set a new record by synchronizing 6,100 neutral atoms in a single quantum array. Published on September 24, 2025 in Nature, the experiment used 12,000 "laser tweezers" — created by splitting a single laser beam — to trap and hold the qubits in superposition.

The team achieved several breakthroughs simultaneously: a 10× improvement in array size (from hundreds to thousands of qubits), coherence times extended to 12.6 seconds (up from just a few seconds), and 99.98% accuracy maintained across the entire array. They also demonstrated a novel "shuttling" technique, moving atoms hundreds of micrometers across the array without losing superposition.

"We can now see a pathway to large error-corrected quantum computers. The building blocks are in place," said Endres in a Caltech statement.

Why Room Temperature Matters

Most quantum computers — including Google's Willow and IBM's systems — use superconducting qubits that must be cooled to near absolute zero (around -273°C), requiring expensive dilution refrigerators. Neutral atom qubits operate at room temperature, dramatically reducing infrastructure costs and complexity.

This architectural advantage could accelerate the path to million-qubit systems. While superconducting approaches face fundamental cooling and wiring constraints as they scale, neutral atom arrays can potentially scale by simply adding more laser tweezers — a far simpler engineering challenge.

Technical Significance

The experiment validated neutral atoms as a viable path to large-scale fault-tolerant quantum computing:

  • 6,100 qubits — largest single quantum array ever demonstrated
  • 12.6s coherence — orders of magnitude longer than superconducting qubits (~100μs)
  • 99.98% accuracy — maintained even at scale, critical for error correction
  • Room temperature — no cryogenic cooling required
  • Shuttling — atoms moved across the array without decoherence, enabling flexible circuit designs

Impact on Bitcoin's Timeline

While 6,100 qubits remain far from the ~4 million logical qubits needed to break Bitcoin's secp256k1 cryptography, this result is significant for several reasons:

Scaling acceleration: The neutral atom approach showed 10× scaling in a single generation. If this pace continues, million-qubit systems could arrive sooner than superconducting roadmaps suggest.
Alternative path: Combined with Microsoft's topological qubits (Majorana 1) and Google's superconducting approach (Willow), this creates a third credible path to large-scale quantum computing. Multiple competing architectures increase the likelihood that at least one will reach cryptographic relevance.
Still far from threat: Even with 6,100 physical qubits, these are not error-corrected logical qubits. Breaking Bitcoin would require millions of logical qubits, each needing ~1,000 physical qubits. The gap remains enormous, but it is narrowing.

The long coherence times (12.6 seconds vs microseconds for superconducting qubits) are particularly noteworthy. Longer coherence means more complex quantum algorithms can be executed, and fewer physical qubits may be needed per logical qubit for error correction — potentially reducing the total qubit count required for cryptographic attacks.
