Bitcoin Launches

2009 — Satoshi Nakamoto launches Bitcoin, unknowingly creating a cryptocurrency vulnerable to quantum attacks fifteen years before they might become relevant.

← Back to Timeline

What Happened

On January 3, 2009, Satoshi Nakamoto mined Bitcoin's genesis block and launched the world's first successful cryptocurrency. Bitcoin's design relied on elliptic curve cryptography (specifically ECDSA with the secp256k1 curve) to secure transactions through digital signatures. This cryptographic choice, standard and secure against classical computers, unknowingly made Bitcoin vulnerable to quantum attacks through Shor's algorithm.

Early Bitcoin transactions used Pay-to-Public-Key (P2PK) outputs, directly exposing public keys on the blockchain. This design choice prioritized simplicity and transparency but created what would later be recognized as maximum quantum vulnerability — public keys sitting exposed on-chain indefinitely, giving quantum computers unlimited time to derive the corresponding private keys.

Satoshi's own coins, estimated at around 1 million BTC, remain in early P2PK addresses that have never been spent. These coins represent some of the most quantum-vulnerable Bitcoin in existence, as their public keys have been visible on the blockchain since 2009, providing quantum computers with over a decade of exposure time to plan attacks.

The Cryptographic Foundation

Bitcoin's choice of ECDSA with secp256k1 was considered state-of-the-art cryptography in 2009. The secp256k1 curve offers 128 bits of security against classical attacks, which was (and remains) computationally infeasible to break. The curve was also chosen for its potential efficiency advantages and was already used in some existing cryptographic systems.

However, Satoshi's design predated widespread awareness of Shor's algorithm's implications for real-world cryptographic systems. While Shor had published his quantum factoring algorithm in 1994, quantum computers remained purely theoretical in 2009. The cryptographic community was just beginning to seriously consider post-quantum alternatives, and NIST's post-quantum standardization process wouldn't begin for another seven years.

The P2PK output type used in early Bitcoin was also a product of its time. Direct public key exposure simplified the scripting system and made transactions more transparent. Later output types like P2PKH (Pay-to-Public-Key-Hash) would hide public keys until spending, but the quantum implications of public key exposure weren't considered during Bitcoin's initial design.

Long-Term Quantum Implications

Bitcoin's 2009 launch created what would later be recognized as a significant quantum vulnerability spanning multiple threat vectors. Approximately 3.4 million BTC remains in early P2PK addresses with permanently exposed public keys, representing about 16% of the total Bitcoin supply. These coins are maximally vulnerable to long-exposure quantum attacks whenever such capabilities become available.

The broader quantum threat to Bitcoin extends beyond just early P2PK coins. All Bitcoin address types rely on elliptic curve cryptography that's vulnerable to Shor's algorithm. Modern address types like P2PKH and P2WPKH hide public keys until spending, creating short-exposure vulnerabilities during transaction broadcast. Taproot addresses (P2TR) reintroduced permanent public key exposure, recreating the quantum vulnerability of early Bitcoin.

Bitcoin's global adoption and immutable blockchain mean that its cryptographic choices from 2009 will affect the network's security indefinitely. Unlike centralized systems that can upgrade cryptography through software updates, Bitcoin requires consensus among thousands of nodes and miners to implement quantum-resistant changes. This creates a unique challenge: Bitcoin must upgrade its cryptography before quantum computers become capable, but the upgrade process itself could take years to implement safely.
