Classical Computers Overturn Quantum Supremacy Claim

May 21, 2026 — Simons Foundation researchers solve a "quantum-only" problem on a laptop using tensor networks. Published in Science.

← Back to Timeline

What Happened

Physicists at the Center for Computational Quantum Physics (CCQ) at the Simons Foundation's Flatiron Institute, along with collaborators at Boston University, used a conventional computer to solve a quantum dynamics problem that had been explicitly claimed to be solvable only by quantum computers.

The problem involved simulating a quantum system of hundreds of interacting qubits arranged in square, cubic, and diamond lattices. In a March 2025 paper published in Science, a group of quantum computing researchers had claimed that they'd calculated the dynamics of this particularly intricate qubit system using a quantum computer — and that classical computers could not match the feat.

The CCQ team proved them wrong. Using cutting-edge tensor network methods and a high-performance software library called ITensor, lead author Joseph Tindall performed many of the initial calculations on a personal laptop. The results, published in Science on May 21, 2026, achieved state-of-the-art accuracies that matched theoretical predictions.

"Whenever we see these kinds of claims, we're always a bit skeptical. Like, 'Did you try this? Did you try that?'"
— Joseph Tindall, CCQ Associate Research Scientist and first author

How They Did It

The key breakthrough was in tensor networks — mathematical data structures that compress enormous quantum wave functions into manageable representations. Tindall likens them to "a zip file for the wave function where you've taken all this information, and you've compressed it into this mathematical data structure full of small tables of numbers interconnected to each other."

The team used a technique called belief propagation, an algorithm from the 1980s recently adapted for quantum systems. While slightly more approximate than some methods, it's vastly cheaper computationally and can handle 3D problems that more sophisticated methods couldn't even begin to tackle.

The simulations captured three-dimensional quantum dynamics using a 3D tensor network — a frontier in computational physics. Co-author Miles Stoudenmire noted that "more sophisticated methods in the past of our field wouldn't be able to even start going for some of these three-dimensional problems, because they're so big."

Impact on Quantum Computing Timeline

This result is significant because it demonstrates that classical computing methods are also improving rapidly — and that quantum supremacy claims must be treated with caution. Each time quantum researchers claim to have solved a problem only quantum computers can handle, classical researchers have consistently found ways to match or come close.

This is part of a recurring pattern:

  • 2019: Google claimed quantum supremacy with Sycamore (53 qubits). IBM later showed classical computers could match it in 2.5 days, not 10,000 years.
  • 2024: Multiple classical algorithms were shown to simulate certain quantum circuits faster than initially believed possible.
  • 2026: CCQ solves a claimed quantum-only 3D dynamics problem on a laptop.

What This Means for Bitcoin

For Bitcoin's quantum threat assessment, this result cuts both ways:

The reassuring angle: Classical computing is not standing still. The gap between what quantum computers can do and what classical computers can simulate continues to narrow in certain domains. Quantum advantage may take longer to become practically relevant than optimistic predictions suggest.
The cautionary angle: This result specifically concerns quantum simulation problems — not cryptographic attacks. Shor's algorithm for breaking elliptic curve cryptography is a fundamentally different type of quantum computation. Tensor networks cannot substitute for the mathematical structure that makes Shor's algorithm effective. The threat to Bitcoin's ECDSA remains unchanged.

The distinction matters: quantum supremacy for simulation tasks and quantum capability for breaking cryptography are separate problems. Progress (or setbacks) in one doesn't necessarily transfer to the other. Bitcoin's security relies on the hardness of the discrete logarithm problem on elliptic curves — a problem where Shor's algorithm provides an exponential speedup that no classical technique has been able to match.
