BIP-360: Pay-to-Merkle-Root Proposed

December 2024 — Bitcoin's first concrete quantum resistance proposal introduces bc1z addresses that never expose public keys.

← Back to Timeline

What Happened

Hunter Beast, Ethan Heilman, and Isabel Foxen Duke formally proposed BIP-360, introducing Pay-to-Merkle-Root (P2MR) as Bitcoin's first concrete quantum resistance mechanism. The proposal outlined a new output type using bc1z addresses that protects against long-exposure quantum attacks by never revealing public keys on the blockchain.

The BIP emerged after months of research and development, building on years of theoretical work on post-quantum Bitcoin defenses. The authors identified a critical vulnerability in existing address types: even unused Bitcoin addresses become vulnerable to quantum attacks once their public keys are exposed during spending, creating a time window for quantum computers to derive private keys.

P2MR solves this by hiding the actual public key inside a Merkle tree commitment, only revealing it at the moment of spending while simultaneously providing a quantum-resistant proof that the spender controls the private key. The proposal included detailed specifications for implementing bc1z addresses as a new Bech32m address format, complete with activation mechanisms and backward compatibility considerations.

Technical Innovation

The core innovation of BIP-360 lies in its commitment-reveal scheme. Instead of directly committing to a public key, P2MR commits to the root of a Merkle tree containing both the public key and a post-quantum signature. When spending, the user reveals both the public key and a proof that it was committed to in the original transaction, along with a post-quantum signature proving ownership.

This approach provides protection against long-exposure quantum attacks (where public keys sit exposed on-chain for extended periods) while maintaining compatibility with Bitcoin's existing UTXO model. The quantum-resistant signatures used in P2MR come from NIST's newly standardized post-quantum cryptography schemes, particularly ML-DSA (Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm).

The proposal cleverly works within Bitcoin's existing OP_SUCCESSx framework, allowing activation without requiring a hard fork. This technical approach reflected lessons learned from previous Bitcoin upgrades like Taproot, prioritizing implementability and consensus-building over theoretical perfection.

Community Reception and Implications

BIP-360's announcement marked a watershed moment for Bitcoin's quantum preparedness discussions. For the first time, the community had a concrete, implementable proposal to evaluate rather than abstract theoretical concerns. The proposal immediately sparked intense technical review and philosophical debate about Bitcoin's quantum future.

The timing proved fortuitous, coinciding with NIST's finalization of post-quantum standards and growing institutional awareness of quantum threats. Major Bitcoin wallet developers began experimental implementations, while mining pools and exchanges started evaluating the operational implications of supporting bc1z addresses.

However, the proposal also highlighted Bitcoin's quantum challenge's complexity. While P2MR addresses long-exposure attacks, it doesn't solve the harder problem of short-exposure attacks during transaction broadcast. Critics argued that partial quantum resistance might create a false sense of security, though supporters countered that incremental progress was better than waiting for perfect solutions.
