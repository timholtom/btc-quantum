Project Eleven Q-Day Prize: Largest Quantum Attack on ECC

April 24, 2026 — Independent researcher breaks a 15-bit elliptic curve key on publicly accessible quantum hardware, 512× larger than the previous record.

← Back to Timeline

What Happened

Quantum security startup Project Eleven awarded its 1 BTC Q-Day Prize (~$78,000) to independent researcher Giancarlo Lelli after he successfully broke a 15-bit elliptic curve key on publicly accessible quantum hardware. Lelli derived a private encryption key from its public counterpart — the exact type of attack that would one day threaten Bitcoin if scaled to 256 bits.

This is the largest public demonstration of a quantum attack on elliptic curve cryptography (ECC) to date. The previous record was a 6-bit key break by researcher Steve Tippeconnic in September 2025 using IBM's 133-qubit quantum computer. Lelli's 15-bit result represents a 512× expansion of the attack surface in just seven months.

Crucially, the winning submission came from an independent researcher working on cloud-accessible quantum hardware — not a national laboratory or a company with proprietary quantum chips. This demonstrates that the barrier to running quantum attacks on real cryptographic primitives is dropping.

Technical Significance

Elliptic curve cryptography is the math that lets a Bitcoin wallet prove it controls funds without revealing its private key. A public key can be visible to everyone, but deriving the corresponding private key is supposed to be computationally infeasible on classical hardware.

Quantum computers running Shor's algorithm challenge that assumption by attacking the underlying mathematical structure (the elliptic curve discrete logarithm problem) that makes ECC secure. Lelli's demonstration proved this attack works on real quantum hardware, not just in simulation.

A 15-bit key has a search space of just 32,767 possibilities — trivially small compared to Bitcoin's 256-bit keys, which have a search space of 2256 (roughly 1077). The result does not mean Bitcoin is close to being cracked. But the Q-Day Prize was designed to track whether quantum attacks on real cryptographic primitives are moving from academic papers into practical hardware experiments — and the answer is yes, at an accelerating pace.

The Trajectory Matters: 6-bit to 15-bit in 7 months. That's a 512× expansion. While exponential scaling challenges remain, and Bitcoin's 256-bit keys are astronomically harder than 15-bit keys, the pace of demonstrated capability is increasing faster than many expected. As Project Eleven CEO Alex Pruden noted: "The resource requirements for this type of attack keep dropping, and the barrier to running it in practice is dropping with them."

Impact on Bitcoin

The concern is sharpest for wallets whose public keys are already visible on-chain. Project Eleven estimates roughly 6.9 million bitcoin sit in such addresses — about one-third of total supply — including Satoshi Nakamoto's estimated 1 million BTC untouched since the network's earliest years. Any quantum computer capable of breaking 256-bit ECC could work through those wallets at leisure, with no time pressure from transaction confirmation windows.

This result adds urgency to ongoing Bitcoin quantum defense efforts:

  • BIP-360 (P2MR): Already running on testnet with 50+ miners and 100K+ transactions
  • StarkWare QSB: Quantum-safe transactions possible today without a fork ($75–$150/tx)
  • Lopp/Papathanasiou proposal: Freeze quantum-vulnerable UTXOs before they can be stolen
  • Coinbase coalition: Industry push for "quantum-proof" custody by late 2026

Google's recent whitepaper estimated that breaking Bitcoin's cryptography could require fewer than 500,000 physical qubits — down from the "millions" often cited. Combined with Caltech/Oratomic's new error correction reducing physical-to-logical qubit ratios, the timeline to cryptographic relevance continues to compress.

Why It Matters

From Theory to Practice: The Q-Day Prize represents a shift in the quantum threat narrative. It's no longer just about theoretical estimates of qubit counts and error rates. Real researchers are now breaking real (if tiny) cryptographic keys on real quantum hardware — and the jumps between milestones are getting larger. The 15-bit break is still 241 bits away from threatening Bitcoin, but the trend line is what matters for planning.
