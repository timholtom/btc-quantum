Paradigm Proposes PACTs: Silent Quantum Rescue for Dormant Bitcoin

May 2026 — Dan Robinson publishes Provable Address-Control Timestamps, offering a free, private way for holders of quantum-vulnerable Bitcoin — including Satoshi's ~1.1M BTC — to prove key ownership before quantum computers arrive.

← Back to Timeline

What Happened

On May 1, 2026, Dan Robinson — a general partner and researcher at crypto venture fund Paradigm — published a proposal for Provable Address-Control Timestamps (PACTs). The design offers a way for dormant Bitcoin holders to privately timestamp proof of wallet ownership today, creating a potential rescue path if Bitcoin ever activates a quantum sunset soft fork that freezes vulnerable addresses.

The proposal directly addresses the most contentious problem in Bitcoin's quantum defense debate: what happens to coins that never migrate to quantum-safe addresses? Under BIP-361 (proposed by Jameson Lopp and Christian Papathanasiou in April 2026), quantum-vulnerable addresses would be frozen on a five-year timeline. That would protect against quantum theft — but it would also lock out every dormant holder who fails to publicly move their coins, including the roughly 1.1 million BTC (~$84 billion) attributed to Satoshi Nakamoto.

How PACTs Work

The core insight is deceptively simple: prove you control a key now, reveal nothing publicly, and only use the proof later if needed.

  1. Generate a secret salt — a 256-bit random value that makes the commitment unique and unguessable
  2. Sign a BIP-322 message — using the standard for proving control of a Bitcoin address without spending from it
  3. Timestamp via OpenTimestamps — the salt and proof are hashed into a commitment, which is anchored into the Bitcoin blockchain through a batched OP_RETURN transaction. This is free and reveals nothing about the underlying address
  4. Store privately — the salt, proof, and timestamp files stay offline with the holder

If Bitcoin later activates a quantum sunset, the protocol could include a rescue path: the holder submits a STARK zero-knowledge proof showing they created their commitment before a cutoff date established prior to CRQC capability. The redemption reveals nothing about which address, which amount, or when the original timestamp was created.

Key Innovation: PACTs use Bitcoin's own architecture — Satoshi designed it as a "distributed timestamp server" — to solve a problem Satoshi's own coins face. The irony is elegant: the system's creator built the tool that could save the creator's coins.

The Satoshi Problem

PACTs make the BIP-361 debate less binary. Instead of choosing between "freeze everything" and "leave everything vulnerable," a rescue path lets holders who prove pre-quantum ownership reclaim their coins even after a sunset.

But PACTs have an irreducible limitation: someone has to create the commitment. If Satoshi is genuinely gone — unable to access those keys — no PACT can be retroactively created. Those coins remain exposed to whichever scenario plays out first: quantum theft or community freeze.

The Open Question: Whether Satoshi will use PACTs is the one thing PACTs cannot answer. If the commitment is created, it proves Satoshi (or whoever controls those keys) is alive and watching. If it isn't, it proves nothing — the keys could be lost, or the holder could simply be unaware.

Technical Requirements

PACTs require no Bitcoin fork today — anyone can create one using existing tools. But the rescue path requires future infrastructure:

  • STARK verification protocol — Bitcoin would need a soft fork adding zero-knowledge proof verification
  • Quantum sunset soft fork — the community must agree to freeze vulnerable addresses (e.g., BIP-361)
  • Standard format — multisig wallets, complex scripts, and hardware wallets all need careful standardization

Robinson acknowledged the design is "illustrative" and needs review from cryptographers, Bitcoin developers, and the broader community. The proposal builds on BIP-361 and references Jeremy Rubin's earlier forum discussions on similar concepts.

Community Response

The proposal generated immediate discussion across the Bitcoin developer community. Coverage appeared within hours on CoinDesk, The Block, and Bitcoin News. Key discussion points include:

  • STARK integration timelines — how long before Bitcoin could verify zero-knowledge proofs?
  • Privacy guarantees — whether the privacy protections would hold in practice
  • Scope limitations — PACTs don't extend cleanly to multisig wallets, complex scripts, or custodial accounts
  • Pre-BIP-32 wallets — Satoshi's addresses predate BIP-32 (2012) and cannot be rescued through BIP-361's deterministic key path

Robinson credited Eli Ben-Sasson (StarkWare), Jameson Lopp, Neha Narula (MIT DCI), and Nic Carter in the acknowledgments — a cross-section of the quantum defense community.

Why This Matters

PACTs represent a philosophical shift in how Bitcoin thinks about quantum defense. Previous proposals focused on forcing migration (BIP-361) or enabling migration (BIP-360, StarkWare QSB). PACTs offer a third path: silent preparation.

The cost of creating a PACT is zero. The risk is near-zero (it reveals nothing). The potential upside — preserving access to billions of dollars in dormant Bitcoin — is enormous. Robinson argues holders should create PACTs once a standard format is agreed upon, regardless of whether Bitcoin ultimately implements a quantum sunset.

The Pragmatic Case: "The cost of creating one is low enough to justify acting once a standard format is agreed upon," Robinson writes. Even if the rescue path is never activated, the downside is a few kilobytes of stored data. The upside is preserving access to generational wealth.
